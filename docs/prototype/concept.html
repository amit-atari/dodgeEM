Dodge 'Em Beyond

The 1980 lane-dodger, rebuilt as a journey. Clear the track, break through the outer wall, and drive out into the next level. Everything on screen is drawn with text characters, except your car.

Research

Four ingredients

Each reference gives us one thing. Anything a reference doesn't give us stays out of the first prototype.

Atari 2600 · 1980

Dodge 'Em

Carla Meninsky's version of Sega's Head On. Four square lanes, gaps in the middle of each side, and a computer car coming the other way.

We take: the lanes, the gaps, the head-on crash car, clearing the dots.
Atari · 2024

qomp2

The Pong ball escapes its game and travels through strange new rooms. You control one thing, and the world keeps changing around it.

We take: escape as the story. One-verb control. The car never stops.
Claude FM look

Living glyph worlds

Landscapes built from dithered ASCII that scrolls and drifts. One small, solid orange sprite. A black "now playing" chip in the corner.

We take: the text-character renderer, the solid hero sprite, the radio framing.
Atari · 2025

Breakout Beyond

A classic reworked with saturated neon, a strong color identity per stage, and loud, clear feedback when something breaks.

We take: the color. Each world owns a hue range. Breaking things feels big.
Core loop

Clear it, crack it, leave it

Every level runs through the same five beats. The demo above plays the whole loop in about 40 seconds.

  1. Clear

    Collect every dot in the arena while the crash car hunts you lane by lane.

  2. Crack

    The last dot cracks one section of the outer wall. The glyphs there flicker white.

  3. Break out

    Reach the outer lane and turn through the crack. The wall bursts into loose characters.

  4. The in-between

    The camera drops from overhead to side-on. A short drive through an open glyph landscape, with the song changing.

  5. Arrive

    A new arena with a new shape, a new hue and one new rule.

Design rules

What holds it together

█

You're the only solid thing

Walls, dots, enemies, sky and ground are all text characters. Your car is solid pixels, so you can always find it at a glance.

↕

One verb: change lanes

The car never stops, like qomp's ball. You only choose where to turn at the gaps. Boost is the one other input.

▓░

The wall you break is the door

The crack appears on a different side in each level, so every exit feels like a small discovery.

⇄

The crash car follows you out

Sometimes it breaks out after you and chases you through the in-between. It works as a recurring rival.

♫

One world, one hue, one song

The now-playing chip names each world's track. Colors shift toward the next world's hue while you travel.

·:+#

Density is information

Denser glyphs mean solid or dangerous. Sparse glyphs are air and background. Players learn to read the screen this way.

Open questions

To settle before the prototype

Does the camera tilt between arenas?

Overhead arenas with side-on in-betweens give the most contrast, but that means two control schemes.

Leaning: yes, with the same up/down input in both views.

Lives, or no failure?

The original uses lives. qomp2 is forgiving and restarts you nearby, which suits a journey.

Leaning: checkpoints per arena, with a classic lives mode.

Two-player mode?

Dodge 'Em let a second player drive the crash car. That could work as a versus mode.

Leaning: after the single-player run is fun.

Platform and tech

An HTML5 canvas glyph renderer is fast to prototype and runs everywhere. A console port can come later.

Leaning: web first, like this demo.

How long is the run?

Five worlds with three or four arenas each, plus in-betweens.

Leaning: a 2–3 hour first playthrough.

Music

Is the radio just framing, or does the beat drive gameplay beyond the Static world?
